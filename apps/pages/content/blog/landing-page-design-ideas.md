Good landing page design ideas begin with the decision your visitor needs to make. Choose references that explain a similar offer, require similar evidence, and lead to a comparable action. Then borrow a specific principle—such as section order or visual hierarchy—rather than copying an entire page that solves a different problem.

A folder of beautiful screenshots can be surprisingly hard to build from. One reference has the typography you want, another has a useful product demonstration, and a third feels more trustworthy. Put all three into a brief without explaining their roles and you have given yourself three competing directions.

This guide turns that collection into a decision. You will end with a one-page brief that names the job, the evidence, the page structure, and what each reference contributes. It works with your own bookmarks or a reference library; you do not need a subscription to use the method.

## 1. Write the visitor's decision before choosing a layout

Start with one sentence: “This page helps **this person** decide whether to **take this action**, because they need **this outcome**.” Be specific enough that two different page concepts could be judged against it.

For an illustrative appointment-booking product, that could be: “This page helps independent tutors decide whether to start a trial because they want students to book lessons without exchanging several messages.” This is a hypothetical brief, not a TastefulKit customer result.

That sentence changes the reference search. A dramatic portfolio homepage might be visually appealing, but it probably does not show how to explain scheduling rules or demonstrate a booking flow. A plainer product page might contain the more useful structural idea.

Write the visitor's likely hesitation beneath the sentence. Is the product unfamiliar? Is the price hard to justify? Does the visitor need to see the output? Do they need to trust the person providing the service? Treat this as a hypothesis to validate with customers, not a fact inferred from the layout.

**Done looks like:** a named audience, one primary action, and one important uncertainty. If you have several unrelated audiences or actions, decide which this page serves before continuing.

## 2. Choose an evidence pattern, not just an aesthetic

Use the following matrix to decide what kind of reference to collect. These are starting hypotheses, not conversion-tested rankings.

| Visitor needs to understand | A useful pattern to study | Evidence to prepare | What can go wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the software actually does | Product demonstration with explanatory labels | A real workflow or output | A decorative dashboard says little about the task |
| Whether a specialist can do the work | Selected work with context | Relevant projects and a clear role | Attractive images hide what the specialist contributed |
| What a service includes | A concrete offer followed by process and scope | Deliverables, boundaries and next steps | Vague promises leave the buyer guessing |
| Whether an event is relevant | Audience, agenda and practical details | Real speakers, timing and location | A striking hero delays essential information |
| Why joining a list is worthwhile | Specific promise with an example | A sample issue or resource | “Stay updated” never explains the benefit |

For the tutor-booking example, the central evidence might be a short sequence: choose a lesson, see available times, confirm the booking. The layout should make that sequence legible. It does not need to resemble a sophisticated enterprise dashboard simply because that dashboard looks expensive.

This is also a useful subtraction exercise. If your references depend on customer logos you do not have permission to use or a photograph you cannot reproduce, mark those parts as unavailable. Do not design a page around imaginary proof and promise to fill it in later.

## 3. Give every reference a job

Collect a small working set rather than another endless inspiration board. Start with three roles: one reference for structure, one for typography, and one for how evidence is presented.

For each reference, complete these three statements:

- **Borrow:** the specific principle that helps this page.
- **Adapt:** what must change for your content, brand or audience.
- **Leave behind:** the attractive feature that does not fit your task.

A useful note reads: “Borrow the short explanation beside each product image. Adapt the images to show lesson booking rather than analytics. Leave behind the animated background because it competes with the interface demonstration.”

An unhelpful note reads: “Make it clean and premium.” Those words can mean generous spacing, low visual density, restrained color, expensive photography, or almost anything else. Replace them with visible decisions.

In TastefulKit, [searching by a visual description](/docs/using-tastefulkit/search/) can help narrow the library. Full reference browsing requires a [membership](/pricing/). For a free starting point, use [Arena](/arena/) to compare landing-page directions or inspect the [global rankings](/rankings/). Neither a winning vote nor a high rank establishes that the page converts better for your audience.

## 4. Translate the references into a section order

Now close the reference tabs briefly and outline your own page in plain text. This prevents one attractive screenshot from silently becoming the specification.

For the hypothetical tutor-booking product, a first outline could be:

1. A headline naming the outcome and a clear trial action.
2. A short, readable booking demonstration.
3. An explanation of availability and scheduling rules.
4. Honest details about setup and what the trial includes.
5. Answers to the most important practical questions.
6. A repeated next step after the visitor has seen the explanation.

This is not a universal formula. A familiar product might need less explanation; a service involving substantial trust may need people and relevant work earlier. The point is that each section resolves an uncertainty rather than filling space in a template.

Read the headings aloud without the body copy. Can someone follow the argument? If the outline says “Welcome,” “Features,” “Benefits,” and “Learn more,” it describes a page skeleton but says almost nothing about the offer. Replace those labels with meaningful working headings before polishing the visual design.

For a more expressive treatment of the same structure, use the constraints in [creative landing page design](/blog/creative-landing-page-design/). The structure should still make sense when the decoration is removed.

## 5. Make the hierarchy explicit

A reference can establish the relationship between elements without supplying exact values. You might observe that the headline dominates the first screen, the supporting explanation is compact, and the main action has stronger contrast than navigation. Those are useful observations.

“Use precisely the same font size and spacing” is less useful unless you have measured them and checked them with your actual content. A headline with different words wraps differently. A longer button label needs more room. A product image with small labels may become unreadable in the same container.

Write hierarchy rules in language a designer or developer can test:

- The page has one visually dominant headline, not several competing display headings.
- The primary action is distinguishable from secondary navigation.
- Product evidence remains readable at the intended display size.
- Related text and images are grouped; unrelated sections have a visible break.
- Important explanations remain understandable without animation.

Then test those rules on a narrow layout. A desktop screenshot does not establish how the original design reflows. The [mobile landing page review](/blog/mobile-landing-page-design/) walks through the missing checks.

## 6. Finish this one-page reference brief

Copy the worksheet below into your project notes. Keep the answers short enough that someone can use them while building, but specific enough to reject the wrong implementation.

| Brief field | Your answer |
|---|---|
| Audience and situation | Who is arriving, and what are they trying to decide? |
| Primary action | What is the one next step this page should make clear? |
| Main uncertainty | What needs explaining or demonstrating before that step? |
| Available evidence | Which real screenshots, examples or facts can you show? |
| Reference roles | Which reference supplies structure, typography or evidence treatment? |
| Borrow / adapt / leave | Which exact decisions belong in each category? |
| Section order | What does each section help the visitor understand? |
| Mobile behavior | What stacks, crops, wraps or changes order? |
| Non-negotiables | What must remain readable, accessible and truthful? |
| Review criteria | What would make you reject the first implementation? |

Here is the completed brief for the tutor-booking example:

- **Audience and action:** independent tutors evaluating a trial, not students booking a lesson.
- **Main uncertainty:** whether setting availability will create more work than it removes.
- **Evidence:** a real booking sequence, plus an honest explanation of setup and scheduling rules.
- **Reference roles:** use one page's labelled product sequence and another's restrained type hierarchy; do not combine both full layouts.
- **Mobile rule:** show the lesson-selection step at a readable size before the explanatory text becomes long.
- **Acceptance:** the visitor can identify who the product serves, understand the booking sequence, and find the trial terms before starting.

For the booking example, a rejection criterion might be: “The lesson-selection image is unreadable on a phone.” That is much more useful than “It doesn't feel right,” because it names a problem the next revision can solve.

If a coding agent will implement the page, turn the completed brief into [a reference handoff with explicit acceptance criteria](/blog/design-references-ai-coding-agents/). Do not give it contradictory screenshots and expect it to infer which one matters most.

## Common mistakes when collecting design ideas

**Selecting by industry alone.** Two businesses in the same industry can have different audiences, price points and next steps. Match the visitor's decision as well as the label.

**Treating popularity as suitability.** A widely admired page may be excellent for its own brand and wrong for your offer. Preference is useful input, not a substitute for a brief or evidence about your users.

**Mixing every reference equally.** If each screenshot contributes a different grid, type system and color palette, the result can lack a coherent hierarchy. Assign roles and make one direction dominant.

**Copying assets instead of learning from decisions.** TastefulKit references belong to their original creators. A screenshot does not give you a licensed template, source code, imagery or brand assets. Build an original page from your own content.

**Stopping at the screenshot.** Follow the original source when possible. Check interactions and narrow-screen behavior; a captured page records one view at one moment.

## What should you do next?

Choose one page you need to build. Write its visitor decision, select a few references with distinct jobs, and complete the brief before opening a design tool. When you compare directions in [Arena](/arena/), add one sentence explaining your preference. That explanation is the part you can carry into your own work.

You have enough inspiration when you can explain what to build, why it fits the visitor's task, and what not to copy. More screenshots are optional; those decisions are not.
